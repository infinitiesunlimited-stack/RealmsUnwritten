# Game Vision

## Document status

- **Role:** Product vision and north star
- **Authority:** Subordinate only to `DESIGN_CONSTITUTION.md`
- **Phase:** Pre-production baseline
- **Setting begins:** Approximately 1370 AD

## High concept

This game is a historically grounded, real-time simulation of settlement, economy, society, government, knowledge, and warfare. The world begins from plausible conditions around 1370, but it does not reenact a fixed history. People, resources, institutions, geography, and political decisions interact to produce a new history on every playthrough.

The player begins as a minor ruler responsible for a small community within a larger political order. Over generations, the player may shape, commission, inherit, negotiate, conquer, lose, delegate, and reform. Growth is neither automatic nor irreversible. A settlement becomes a civilization through accumulated people, material capacity, knowledge, institutions, and remembered events.

The player manages and shapes a civilization while the population physically lives, develops, works, and fights within it. That is neither a conventional RTS in which the player places and controls every ordinary building and military unit, nor a passive simulation in which the player has little influence over development and warfare.

## Player fantasy

The player should feel that they are guiding a real place through time rather than optimizing an abstract production board or painting cottages onto a grid. A loaf of bread should imply land, weather, labor, transport, storage, milling, baking, ownership, and access. A weapon should imply ore, fuel, expertise, tools, workshops, logistics, and users. A thriving city should be the result of families and institutions persisting across generations, not a roster of buildings the player stamped down one by one.

The player operates at civilization, institutional, and planning scale: roads, land release and reservation, settlement extent, markets, mills, bridges, defenses, major civic or religious works, and planning policy, according to the civilization's authority. Ordinary households generally develop their own property. Direct placement of major works remains legitimate; requiring the player to place every cottage, barn, shed, or garden does not. The player is not an individual battlefield character. People and formations execute the commands that authority issues.

The desired emotional arc is:

1. **Stewardship:** Understand a few families, their property, and their immediate needs.
2. **Interdependence:** Build reliable local production and exchange without losing sight of physical constraints.
3. **Continuity:** Watch people age, households branch, buildings endure, and local customs emerge.
4. **Authority:** Gain responsibilities and powers inside political structures that already exist.
5. **Delegation:** Create institutions capable of governing complexity without erasing it.
6. **Historical consequence:** See migrations, wars, discoveries, failures, and inherited infrastructure reshape later generations.

## Foundational promise

The calendar advances history; the simulation advances civilization.

The year determines which developments are historically plausible, not which developments a society automatically owns. Adoption depends on the presence and interaction of people, knowledge, institutions, materials, productive capability, and experience. A society may lead, follow, specialize, stagnate, recover, or lose knowledge.

## Core pillars

### People have continuity

People are persistent simulation entities with origins, age, kinship, household membership, occupation, skills, knowledge, possessions, residence, and cultural background. They are born, migrate, form relationships and households, work, learn, age, and die. Population is never a building output.

### Households make settlements

The household is the central social and economic unit. It connects people to property, shelter, productive activity, consumption, inheritance, wealth, and migration. Household prosperity should become visible in how a property is occupied and modified over time. Ordinary domestic structures accumulate because households need them and can afford them within land, access, custom, and law—not because the player issued a place-building order for each one, and not because the simulation sprawls without constraint.

### Land and location matter

Agriculture, forestry, extraction, construction, transport, and defense use physical space. Fields have measurable area and environmental history. Roads, paths, plots, property boundaries, existing structures, terrain, waterways, and major institutions shape settlement form. Distance and terrain impose costs instead of merely decorating the map. Population growth does not automatically equal outward expansion: vacant property, recoverable structures, infill, and redevelopment compete with new construction according to circumstance. Organic growth is constrained by inherited geography and prior development; it is not uncontrolled sprawl.

### Goods have a place and a path

Material goods exist in inventories attached to people, vehicles, buildings, sites, fields, and markets. Production changes located inputs into located outputs. Moving goods is work that takes time and capacity. Strategic shortages should be traceable to physical causes.

### Knowledge has carriers

Knowledge exists in people, artifacts, texts, practices, and institutions. It spreads through contact and teaching, can be applied only where supporting capability exists, and can be lost. There is no spend-points-to-unlock technology tree. Military service, when it exists, is part of a persistent person's history: experience, skills, knowledge, prestige, and household standing may change, and veterans may return knowledge to civilian society.

### Development is gradual and path-dependent

New methods coexist with old ones. Adoption is constrained by tooling, training, capital, materials, risk, institutional support, and local value. Old buildings and practices remain until something in the world changes them.

### Politics surrounds the player

The player is not initially a sovereign abstraction. Emperors, kings, sultans, princes, counts, orders, governors, councils, and religious authorities already claim rights and obligations. Political advancement is a simulated process of title, territory, legitimacy, service, negotiation, conflict, and institutional change.

### War is legible and competitive

Military outcomes arise from equipment, formation, training, morale, leadership, terrain, logistics, experience, and economic capacity. Historical starting asymmetries matter, but no culture receives a predetermined destiny, permanent winner status, or arbitrary combat bonus detached from simulated cause.

The army is not a separate game. Forces arise from the same people, goods, animals, institutions, and logistical capacity as civilian life. A person who musters is unavailable at home; a person who returns is still that person; a person who dies is lost to household and civilization. Civilization depth and tactical battle depth are both first-class: when armies physically meet, battles should be real engagements, not a single abstract strength comparison, and not a disconnected combat layer with its own population and equipment.

### The world remembers

People, structures, property, institutions, objects, and events retain provenance. A building can outlive its builder. Abandonment is not deletion, and age is not automatic destruction: vacant, neglected, or ruined fabric may be restored, reused, or left as traces. Settlements may grow, contract, and recover on the same ground. A refugee family can alter a town generations later. A captured manuscript can seed a craft tradition. Military service is part of a persistent person's history. The present should be readable as the accumulated result of the past.

## Historical grounding and divergence

Starting data should represent defensible regional conditions circa 1370: political relationships, settlement patterns, crops, resources, construction traditions, military practices, institutions, and known techniques. Research must distinguish well-supported fact, scholarly interpretation, and necessary design approximation.

Once play begins, causality takes priority over chronology. Historical events may enter as pressures or opportunities only when their prerequisites and delivery mechanisms make sense. Their historical outcomes are never forced. For example, Constantinople is not destined to fall, the Teutonic Order is not destined to decline, and no Balkan, Franconian, Ottoman, or Baltic polity is assigned an inevitable trajectory.

## Cultural and regional identity

Franconian/Holy Roman, Teutonic/Baltic Frontier, Serbian/Balkan, and Ottoman regions should begin with different circumstances rather than mirrored rosters plus bonuses. Differences should emerge from geography, crops, settlement forms, institutions, legal practices, military traditions, trade connections, architecture, material access, and starting knowledge.

Culture is neither biological destiny nor a fixed gameplay class. Communities can borrow, mix, preserve, reject, or transform practices through real contact and incentives.

## Scale and control

At village scale, direct attention to people, plots, and deliveries is appropriate. At regional scale, the same underlying concerns remain, but institutions and delegated offices mediate the player's control. Growth should unlock better governance tools, not an ever-growing queue of individual chores, and not an ever-growing queue of ordinary buildings to place by hand.

These are different resolutions of one world:

```text
CIVILIZATION SCALE
Kingdom / region / politics / economy / war
        ↓
SETTLEMENT SCALE
Households / production / construction / trade
        ↓
BATTLE SCALE
People / formations / equipment / terrain / command
```

Simulation fidelity may use different update frequencies and representations according to relevance, but it must preserve authoritative identity, ownership, quantities, and consequential state. A battle may raise local detail; it must not replace the settlement's people or goods with a parallel model. Presentation detail may disappear with distance; causality may not.

## Prototype 0.1 promise

The first playable proof is deliberately narrow: one Franconian settlement, about five households and 20–30 people, one road, household properties, one forest, one wheat farm, storage, a mill, a bakery, and a market.

It proves one complete physical chain:

> A family immigrates, receives property, cultivates and harvests wheat, transports it to storage, sends it to the mill, turns flour into bread at the bakery, delivers bread to the market, and obtains food for a household.

Basic logging supports the settlement and validates a second form of physical work and transport. Combat, multiple civilizations, regional politics, and the advanced knowledge model are intentionally absent.

## Experience targets

The game should be:

- **Causally legible:** The player can discover why a person, household, or production chain is succeeding or failing.
- **Historically plausible:** Systems and content respect the material and institutional realities of their time and place.
- **Emergent:** Outcomes follow simulated conditions rather than protected scripts.
- **Persistent:** Decisions leave durable social and physical evidence.
- **Scalable:** Detail remains meaningful as control shifts from direct action to delegation.
- **Strategically readable:** Economic and military choices produce understandable tradeoffs suitable for competitive play.
- **Data-informed:** Content variations should normally be expressed as data and rules, not duplicated code paths.

## Explicit non-goals

The game is not:

- A scripted reenactment of a canonical timeline.
- A conventional age-progression RTS.
- A conventional RTS in which the player must place and control every ordinary building and military unit.
- A passive sandbox in which the player cannot commission major works or shape development and war.
- A city painter where inhabitants are decorative traffic, or a procedural city that sprawls without property, access, or institutional constraint.
- A production game with invisible global resource pools, including an abstract army supply pool disconnected from located goods.
- A culture roster balanced through arbitrary permanent bonuses alone.
- A disposable military population spawned apart from persistent people.
- A battle layer that is only an abstract army-strength comparison, or that uses incompatible people, equipment, or provenance.
- A promise to simulate every action at identical resolution everywhere.
- A license to add detail that does not create meaningful causes, choices, or consequences.

## Long-term success criteria

The vision is achieved when players can inspect a later settlement and explain how its current form arose: who came there, who learned what, which properties changed hands, where its food and arms originated, which institutions manage it, what conflicts altered it, and why its path differs from both real history and another playthrough.
